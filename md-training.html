A Practical Guide to Biomolecular Simulation and CHARMM

assembled by Jianhan Chen for internal use
All rights reserved, 2009

Overview: Effective biomolecular simulation requires a range of background that might be grouped into four key areas:

  1. Linux/Unix operation system: scripting languages and common tools
  2. Molecular mechanics: theories and algorithms
  3. CHARMM: principles and practice
  4. The science: chemistry, physics and biology (aka: biophysics and biochemistry).
Items 1 and 3 are important for correct and efficient design and implementation of computational experiments (i.e., simulations) and subsequent analysis. The importance of these technical aspects can be easily over-emphasized, especially for beginners. However, they are far from sufficient for successful research in biomolecular modeling. Instead, in-depth understanding of the simulation methodologies (both pros and cons), and particularly, the science, is critical. Building up the necessary background in all these areas takes time. It demands diligence and persistence, and benefits from good guidance.

To young graduate students (and postdoctoral scholars new to the field): please remember to read papers, tons of them! You should feel bad if you are not reading as least a dozen of papers every week! Literature has remained the main source for up-to-date information on state-of-the-art of virtually every research subject. This guide focuses heavily on the technical aspects (which ironically are "less" important as stated above), and only touches on some basic methodological and scientific aspects.

In the following, I will provide/assemble online and local materials for each of the items listed above. While this document will serve as a reasonable starting point for learning and is a good reference, I want to remind you that Google/Internet is your best friend when it comes to specific technical questions. As you will notice, this guide relies heavily on available resources on the Internet.

2. Molecular Mechanics

2.1. Basic principles: The basic idea of molecular mechanics (MM) is very simple: the molecule consists of classical particles (e.g., atoms with well defined mass, positions and velocities at any given moment of time). There is no explicit electron like in quantum mechanics ("reality") and no chemistry. The interactions between these classical particles are generally divided into bonded and non-bonded interactions depending on whether the interacting particles are linked by no greater than three chemical bonds. Such simplification requires heavy parameterization based on both experimental data and quantum mechanical calculations (which leads to so-called empirical force fields), but allows description of large molecules like proteins. The motion of classical particles is described by the Newton's Equations of Motion, and this is the basis of so-called molecular dynamics (MD) simulations (conformational sampling). Force field and sampling are two basic components of biomolecular modeling. The basic principles of MM are so simple, such that educated students can write an MD code in less than an hour. Nonetheless, substantial complications do arise in practical simulation of biomolecules. These complications mostly have to do with achieving sufficient accuracy and computational efficiency such that meaningful biochemical and biophysical questions can be addressed.

2.2. Textbooks and online readings: A few "classical" textbooks expand on basic MM/MD theories and algorithms, including "Understanding Molecular Simulations" by Frankel and Smit, "Computer Simulations of Liquids" by Allen and Tildesley, and "Proteins: A Theoretical Perspective of Dynamics, Structure and Thermodynamics" by Brooks, Karplus and Pettitt. These books are also useful for understanding the theories behind the interpretation of simulation trajectories. Statistical mechanics (or statistical thermodynamics) links the simulated microscopic ensembles to macroscopic measurables and provides the basis for most common analysis of classical simulations. The classical textbooks on statistical mechanics include McQuarrie's "Statistical Mechanics" (comprehensive and in-depth) or an expanded/simplified version for undergraduate-level courses, "Physical Chemistry: A Molecular Approach" by McQuarrie and Simon. David Chandler's short book on "Introduction to Modern Statistical Mechanics" is easy to read but yet with good breadth and depth. I have all these books in my office for loan. Some interesting overviews can also be found online, such as: (if you can only read one of them, read the first one)

3. CHARMM Simulation Package

3.1. Overview. CHARMM is one of the major general-purpose (bio)molecular simulation packages. We mainly use CHARMM in our lab. Compared to Amber, Gromacs, Namd and others, CHARMM is unique in several ways. First, CHARMM provides modules that need to be "assembled" by the user in the form of "input scripts" to carry out simulation and analysis, while all the other programs pre-assemble the flows and only allow users to specify certain parameters (e.g., length of simulation, nonbond options, etc). Such a philosophy provides great flexibility and can be very useful in research (where we often need to carry out non-standard operations), but can also make it more difficult and error-prone for beginners. Second, CHARMM contains one of the largest collection of analysis tools. Combined with the scripting ability, albeit limited, CHARMM can be programmed to do seemingly complicated analysis with a little effort. Most other software require usage of a suite of independent programs to carry out different analysis, with the exception of NAMD/VMD. Third, CHARMM is primarily a research tool that is used and developed by many research groups around the world. With some training, one can easily modify and expand CHARMM for your specific purposes. The flip side of this nature is that tools in CHARMM are not all well documented or user friendly.

Frequently cited limitations of CHARMM include 1) not user friendly (documents difficult to read), 2) steep learning curve, and 3) does not scale well on multiple processors (e.g., >16 CPUs). There are multiple ongoing efforts to resolve all these limitations. Particularly in terms of CHARMM training, multiple workshops have been offered (e.g., MMTSB/CTBP workshops) and several resources are available for providing examples/tutorials to get you started (e.g., CHARMM GUI, MMTSB, CHARMM forum). Charles Brooks III is also leading an NIH-supported effort to modernize CHARMM and improve the computational efficiency across the spectrum.

Albeit limited and often difficult to understand for beginners, I strongly suggest everyone to read through the core document pages at least once when learning charmm. One should also read through related documents whenever learning to use new functionalities in CHARMM. This habit allows one to become more familiar with what is available and such familiarity will be very useful in thinking about how to setup new simulations or analyze the trajectories. There are many online versions of CHARMM documentation, such this one hosted in Michigan. In particular, the following categorized one can be very handy for beginners: http://brooks.chem.lsa.umich.edu/content/charmm_docs/c32docs/c32a1/category.html.

3.4. MMTSB Tool Set: the toolset provides many convenient functionalities for simple analysis. The most frequently used tools of MMTSB in our lab is the replica exchange simulation support (aarex.pl and related tools). In addition, we have used the tool set quite extensively for clustering analysis (enscluster.pl) and pdb structure manipulation (convpdb.pl). My recommendation is that try to learn CHARMM well and do most of the "standard" analysis in CHARMM. Use the tool set when you need to get some numbers (such as RMSD between two structures) quickly. MMTSB Tool Set has some reasonable documentation (http://feig.bch.msu.edu/mmtsb/Main_Page). The MMTSB/CTBP workshop also contains many tutorials/examples on how to use the tool set in various ways. Also see Michael Feig's lecture note for an overview.

4. Biophysics and Biochemistry

This is such a large topic and I do not even know where to start. In general, the papers that I give you to read at the beginning of your projects will be good starting points to build up the necessary scientific background. Your course training is also a critical component that builds the knowledge base of your research work. While the graduate program might not require you to take a large number of courses, I encourage to take advantage of many graduate or high-level undergraduate courses offered on relevant topics. This is the most efficient way of obtaining formal and systematic training on the corresponding subject matter. Based on my personal experience, while self-study is a critical ability in research, it is often not effective in terms of basic training. For example, if you want to learn statistical mechanics, the best way is simply to take a course on statistical mechanics (that is taught by a good instructor). Do not take your courses lightly! Take advantage of the (precious) opportunities to refresh, refine and expand your knowledge base.
